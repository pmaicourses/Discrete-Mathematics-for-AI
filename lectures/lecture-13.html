03 · A Crossing Can Be a Drawing Choice

A graph is planar if it can be drawn so edges meet only at their shared endpoints, with no unintended crossings. A crossing in one sketch does not prove the graph is nonplanar: the same vertices and edges may be redrawn.

The four-cycle has edges AB, BC, CD, DA. The left sketch below crosses two edges; the right sketch keeps exactly the same edge list but has no crossing. The intersection in the first sketch is not a vertex: there is no dot and the edge list contains no junction there.

Quick check: Adding a dot at the crossing would create a different graph with another vertex and different edge segments. Do not silently change the graph while redrawing it.

04 · Count Regions in a Connected Planar Drawing

In a crossing-free drawing, a face is a region bounded by the drawing. Count the unbounded outside region as one face. For a simple connected planar graph, Euler's formula states:

V−E+F=2.

The four-cycle has V=4 vertices and E=4 edges. Its crossing-free square has one inside face and one outside face, so F=2, and 4−4+2=2.

The Week 13 campus drawing

Its five buildings and six walkways form a connected graph drawn without crossings. The drawing has two bounded faces: the triangle L–H–S–L and the remaining region L–S–H–C–G–L. Include the outside face to get F=3. Thus V−E+F=5−6+3=2.

Use this equation as a check after you have a valid simple connected crossing-free drawing. The formula does not make a crossing disappear and does not apply in this stated form to an arbitrary disconnected sketch.

05 · A Time Slot Is a Color

Suppose two examinations share students and therefore cannot run at the same time. Represent each exam by a vertex and each stated conflict by an edge. Give every vertex a time slot, shown as a color. A proper vertex coloring assigns different colors to the two endpoints of every edge.

Quick check: Different slots for every exam would be valid but could waste slots. The mathematical aim here is a valid assignment using the available slots, then a justified minimum for this small graph.

07 · Predict the Minimum, Then Construct a Schedule

Exams A, B and C are pairwise adjacent, so none of them may share a slot with either of the other two. At least three slots are necessary. This is a lower bound: it rules out two slots, but we must still construct a valid three-slot assignment.

Predict → Test → Explain: Try changing D to Slot 3. Which edge breaks? It is CD, because C already uses Slot 3. Try changing E to Slot 1: EA breaks because A uses Slot 1, and DE also breaks when D is in Slot 1.

09 · Visit Every Vertex Exactly Once

A Hamiltonian path visits every vertex exactly once, with no requirement to use every edge. A Hamiltonian cycle visits every vertex exactly once before returning to its starting vertex. The start appears again only to close the cycle. These ideas ask about vertices; Euler routes ask about edges.

Route goalWhat must appear once?May other object repeat or remain unused?
Euler trailEvery edgeVertices may repeat; no edge remains unused.
Hamiltonian pathEvery vertexSome edges may remain unused.
Hamiltonian cycleEvery vertex before returnReturns to start; some edges may remain unused.

There is no direct analogue here of the simple Euler odd-degree test that decides Hamiltonian paths in general. On our small graph, we justify a Hamiltonian route by writing it and checking every consecutive edge and every vertex.

Quick check: A route through all five buildings that repeats one building before it has visited all five is not a Hamiltonian path, even if it is a valid walk.

10 · Major Case Study 4 Concludes: Campus Inspection and Delivery Robot

Scenario → Understand → Represent → Reason → Test → Interpret. Continue with the same Week 13 campus graph. Buildings are L,H,C,G,S; walkways are LH, HC, CG, GL, LS, SH. All are two-way and open. The drawing below has no unintended crossings.

Mission A · Inspect every walkway

Degrees are 3,3,2,2,2 at L,H,C,G,S. The connected graph has exactly two odd vertices L and H. An open Euler trail is L–H–C–G–L–S–H; it uses all six edges once. It revisits buildings, and it cannot be a closed Euler circuit.

Mission B · Visit every building

A Hamiltonian path is L–S–H–C–G. It visits L,S,H,C,G exactly once through walkways LS, SH, HC, CG. A Hamiltonian cycle is L–S–H–C–G–L; it uses LS, SH, HC, CG, GL and returns to L. The walkway LH is unused in both building-visit routes, which is allowed for this mission.

Interpret the robot's decision

The robot can satisfy each task on its own, but the written routes certify different requirements. An inspection trail may revisit a building; a building-visit cycle may leave a walkway uninspected. Travel times, directions, closures and battery constraints have not been modelled. We have exhibited routes for this specific small graph, not a universal Hamiltonian test.

11 · One Graph, Two Precise Decisions

QuestionValid routeVerification
Inspect each walkway once?L–H–C–G–L–S–HSix different edges used; L and H are odd endpoints.
Visit each building once?L–S–H–C–GFive distinct buildings visited; four walkways used.
Visit each building, then return?L–S–H–C–G–LFive distinct buildings before return; LH unused.

The Euler degree test supports the first question for connected graphs. It does not decide the second or third question. For the campus case we verify the Hamiltonian routes directly from the named edge list.

12 · Optional Colab: Check a Proposed Schedule

Self-study · mathematics first Construct and audit a three-slot coloring on paper. Then use a short checker for the six stated conflict edges. This cell reuses Week 13's dictionary of keys and values and an already familiar for loop.

conflicts = [("A", "B"), ("A", "C"), ("B", "C"),
             ("C", "D"), ("D", "E"), ("E", "A")]
slot = {"A": 1, "B": 2, "C": 3, "D": 1, "E": 2}
for left, right in conflicts:
    print(left, right, slot[left] != slot[right])

Each pair in conflicts is one edge. slot[left] looks up that exam's assigned slot. != asks whether two conflicting exams have different slots. Predict six True results. Change slot["D"] to 3 and predict which edge prints False before running again.

This code checks the supplied assignment against the listed conflicts. It does not find an optimal schedule or detect conflicts absent from the input.

14 · Common Mistakes

Tempting moveRepair
Declare a graph nonplanar because one sketch has crossings.Try a different drawing while preserving the same vertices and edges.
Count only bounded regions in Euler's formula.Include the unbounded outside face.
Use V−E+F=2 on any disconnected sketch.This week's form assumes a simple connected planar drawing.
Give conflicting exams the same slot.Check every edge's endpoints, not only the triangle.
Call a proper three-slot assignment “minimum” without a reason.The triangle proves at least three; the assignment proves at most three.
Use the Euler odd-degree test to decide Hamiltonian routes.The degree test concerns every edge; inspect vertex visits separately.

15 · Summary

  • A graph is planar if some drawing has no unintended edge crossings. A crossed sketch can sometimes be redrawn.
  • For a simple connected planar drawing, count the outside face and check V−E+F=2.
  • A proper vertex coloring gives adjacent vertices different colors. In the exam model, adjacent exams cannot share a time slot.
  • A Hamiltonian path visits every vertex once; a Hamiltonian cycle returns to its start after visiting every vertex once. Edges may remain unused.
  • The campus graph has an open Euler inspection trail and a Hamiltonian building-visit cycle. Each route answers its own precise question.

Selected Solutions

Schedule Studio

Two slots fail because A, B and C conflict pairwise. One valid coloring is A=1, B=2, C=3, D=1, E=2. Audit AB: 1/2, AC: 1/3, BC: 2/3, CD: 3/1, DE: 1/2, EA: 2/1. All six conflicting pairs have different slots. Therefore exactly three slots are required.

1 · Redrawing a cycle

Draw A–B–C–D–A as a square. It has V=4,E=4,F=2 (one inside, one outside); 4−4+2=2.

2 · Campus faces

The bounded triangle L–H–S–L, the bounded region L–S–H–C–G–L, and the outside give F=3. With V=5,E=6, 5−6+3=2.

3–4 · Time slots

The A–B–C triangle forces three distinct slots; the assignment in Schedule Studio proves three suffice. In A=1,B=2,C=3,D=3,E=2, CD is the sole conflict (C and D both in Slot 3). Changing D to Slot 1 repairs it: CD becomes 3/1 and DE becomes 1/2.

5 · Building visits

L–S–H–C–G is a Hamiltonian path and leaves LH and GL unused. L–S–H–C–G–L is a Hamiltonian cycle and leaves LH unused.

6 · Inspection versus visit

L–H–C–G–L–S–H uses LH, HC, CG, GL, LS, SH exactly once. It repeats L and H, so it is not a Hamiltonian path.

7 · Invalid inference

The campus graph has odd-degree vertices L and H, so it has no Euler circuit. Yet L–S–H–C–G–L is a Hamiltonian cycle. The Euler degree test cannot rule it out.
