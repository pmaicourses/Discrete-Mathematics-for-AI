01 · Opening Scenario

You enter a campus study room. A lamp is controlled by a motion sensor. The posted rule says: “If motion is detected, the lamp turns on.” You walk in and see the lamp on. Can you conclude that motion was detected?

Pause before reading on. The lamp may have been switched on manually. Seeing the lamp on does not, by itself, tell us why it is on. But if the sensor detected motion and the posted rule is operating as stated, then the lamp must be on. This difference between a rule and what can be concluded from an observation is the start of mathematical reasoning.

First thought: A useful answer gives a reason and states its assumptions. “The lamp is on, so motion was detected” is not justified by the posted rule.

Reasoning Lens · five questions to reuse all semester

  1. GIVEN: The posted rule says that detected motion requires the lamp to turn on; we observe the lamp on.
  2. DECIDE: Does the observation establish that motion occurred?
  3. REPRESENT: Let P mean motion was detected and Q mean the lamp is on. The rule is P → Q; the observation is Q.
  4. FOLLOWS: If P were also known, the rule would require Q. The observation is consistent with the rule.
  5. DOES NOT FOLLOW: We cannot infer P from Q alone. A manual switch is a possible alternative.

We will reuse these five questions for proofs, sets, counting, graphs, and trees.

02 · Why This Matters

Mathematical reasoning asks: What do we know? What follows from it? What remains uncertain? Many systems make decisions from explicit conditions: a gate opens when an authorized card is present, a reminder is sent when an appointment is due, or an application displays a warning when two conditions occur together. Logic lets us describe and inspect such conditions precisely.

Later in this course we will prove statements, count possible cases, and study networks. Each task depends on distinguishing a statement from a question, interpreting the word “if,” and checking every possible truth-value case. These are skills you can learn without previous computing experience.

04 · Intuitive Introduction

Consider four sentences: (a) “The study room lamp is on.” (b) “Please turn on the lamp.” (c) “Is the lamp on?” (d) “The room has 27 chairs.” Sentences (a) and (d) can be true or false in a specified situation, even if we do not yet know which. Sentence (b) is a request; (c) is a question. A request and a question are not true or false in the required sense.

We can combine ordinary statements. If P means “motion is detected” and Q means “the lamp is on,” then “motion is detected and the lamp is on” needs both parts; “motion is detected or the lamp is on” needs at least one part. The conditional “if motion is detected, then the lamp is on” imposes a requirement only when motion is detected.

Think Carefully · three different categories

False proposition: “8 is an odd number” is a statement with a definite truth value, and that value is false. It is still a proposition. Non-proposition: “Please open the door” is a request; “Is the server active?” is a question. Neither asserts something true or false. Open statement: x > 5 depends on an unspecified x, so it has no definite truth value yet. Specifying x=7 would make a true proposition. Week 2 will develop statements with variables further.

05 · Mathematical Concepts

Reasoning from stated information

Begin by writing the rule and the observation separately. In the opening scenario the rule is P → Q. If we also know P, we may expect Q. If we know only Q, the rule does not tell us whether P occurred. In Week 2, we will use facts and rules to decide what conclusions actually follow; for now, focus on careful interpretation.

Four basic ways to combine propositions

Negation: ¬P means “not P.” Its truth value is the opposite of P.
Conjunction: P ∧ Q means “P and Q.” Both must be true.
Disjunction: P ∨ Q means “P or Q.” At least one must be true; both may be true.
Implication: P → Q means “if P, then Q.” It fails precisely when P is true and Q is false.

D · Implication: a conditional promise

Imagine a clear promise: “If you submit the assignment by Friday, a confirmation will be sent.” Let P mean submitted by Friday and Q mean confirmation sent. The promise is P → Q. It is broken precisely when the student submits on time but receives no confirmation: P=T, Q=F.

If the student does not submit on time, the promise does not specify whether a confirmation can be sent for another reason. For this promise, either value of Q is consistent when P=F. This is why the two false-premise rows count as true for the conditional statement; they do not claim that the system actually sent anything.

PQP → QPromise status
TTTKept
TFFBroken
FTTNo requirement on Q
FFTNo requirement on Q

Misconception: Receiving a confirmation does not prove timely submission. That would require the converse, Q → P, a different rule. Quick check: Submission is late, confirmation is sent. Is the stated promise broken?

Reveal explanation

No. The original promise only commits to what happens after a timely submission.

When more than one connective appears, parentheses tell us what is grouped: (P ∧ Q) → R is a rule about both P and Q together. Do not silently change the grouping.

06 · Formal Definitions and Notation

A proposition is a declarative statement that, in a specified context, is either true or false. We may not know its truth value, but it still has one. The value of a compound proposition depends on the values of its components.

SymbolNameWhen truePlain-language reading
¬PNegationWhen P is falseNot P
P ∧ QConjunctionBoth are trueP and Q
P ∨ QInclusive disjunctionAt least one is trueP or Q, possibly both
P → QImplicationEvery case except P=T, Q=FIf P, then Q
Scope of “or”: In standard propositional logic, ∨ is inclusive. If a real policy permits exactly one alternative, the policy needs an additional constraint; the symbol ∨ alone does not express “exactly one.”

08 · Step-by-Step Worked Examples

Level 1 · simple intuition

Is each sentence a proposition?

“7 is an odd number” has a truth value (true). “Please write 7” is a command and has no truth value. “The number x is odd” has an unspecified variable; its truth value depends on a chosen value of x, so it is not yet a proposition in this context. “The number 8 is odd” is a proposition (false).

Reasoning: The test is whether the sentence can be assigned a truth value, not whether we already know or like that value.

Example 2 · negation

Is the lab open?

Understand: The timetable states, “The lab is open at 2 p.m. on Tuesday.” Represent: Call this proposition L; its negation is ¬L. Solve: If the timetable says the lab is closed at that time, then L=F and ¬L=T. Interpret: The lab could be open at other times. The negation says nothing about them.

Level 2 · real system

Campus room access

Let A mean “the card is authorized” and B mean “the room is within opening hours.” The policy permits entry only if both conditions hold: A ∧ B.

  1. If the card is authorized (A=T) but the building is closed (B=F), then A ∧ B=F.
  2. If both are true, A ∧ B=T.
  3. “Not authorized” is ¬A; it is true exactly when A=F.

Interpretation: The notation makes the access rule testable in all four possible input cases.

Compare AND and OR: If either an authorized card or a staff override can admit a person, let S mean “staff override is active.” Then the alternative entry condition is A ∨ S; when both are true, the inclusive OR remains true. The two policies answer different questions.

Level 3 · beginner computing example

Does a lamp rule work in every observed case?

Let P mean “sensor detected motion” and Q mean “lamp is on.” The rule is P → Q. Suppose a log has these four entries:

LogMotion PLamp QRule P → QReason
1TTTRequired lamp state occurs.
2TFFMotion occurred, but lamp stayed off.
3FTTManual switching is possible. This rule places no requirement on Q in this case.
4FFTThis rule places no requirement on Q in this case.

Reasoning: Check the premise first. If it is true, the conclusion must be true. This does not assert that a physically faulty lamp “makes the rule true”; the false row identifies a violation of the stated rule.

Converse check: Q → P would say that every time the lamp is on, motion was detected. Log 3, with Q=T, P=F, refutes that converse while satisfying the original rule. Our question is always what the given rule actually says.

Example 5 · translate a system rule

When should a reminder appear?

Understand: “If an appointment is tomorrow and reminders are enabled, show a reminder.” Represent: A means appointment tomorrow; E means reminders enabled; R means reminder shown. The full rule is (A ∧ E) → R. Solve: For A=T, E=T, R=F, the premise A ∧ E=T, so the rule is false. Interpret: The system failed the stated requirement in that case. If E=F, the rule does not require a reminder.

09 · Real-System Scenario Examples

Scheduling two conditions

Let R mean “a classroom is free” and I mean “the instructor is free.” A meeting can proceed only if R ∧ I. If either condition fails, the combined condition is false. This illustrates why “and” is stricter than “or.”

Two ways to receive a message

Let E mean “email delivery succeeds” and S mean “SMS delivery succeeds.” “At least one channel succeeds” is E ∨ S. It remains true if both succeed. If a contract says “exactly one,” that is a different requirement.

A condition is not its converse

“If the alarm test button is pressed, a sound plays” is T → S. “If a sound plays, the test button was pressed” is S → T. An actual alarm can sound for other reasons, so the second statement need not follow from the first.

10 · AI/Computing Connection

A rule-based decision system evaluates clearly stated conditions. For example, “If the user has requested an accessible route and an accessible route exists, then display it” can be written using propositions about the request, availability, and display. Logic helps us inspect which combinations of input conditions a rule addresses and where information is missing.

This is one simple kind of decision logic, not a description of how every AI system works. Data-driven systems can also contain Boolean checks and stated policies, but a truth table does not by itself make a program intelligent or ensure that its inputs are accurate. In Week 2 we will ask whether conclusions actually follow from multiple rules and facts.

INPUTS
Authorized card A=T
Opening hours H=T
LOGICAL CONDITION
A ∧ H = T
DECISION
Access allowed under this rule

This is a simple rule-based decision. Modern AI systems can use much more complex methods, while Boolean checks remain useful in software and decision systems around them. The rule still depends on accurate inputs and a clearly stated policy.

13 · Check Your Understanding

  1. Can a proposition be false? Give an example.
  2. When are P ∧ Q and P ∨ Q different?
  3. What is the only false case of P → Q?
  4. If the lamp is on, does P → Q alone prove that motion was detected?
Reveal concise answers

1. Yes: “8 is odd” is a false proposition. 2. When exactly one of P,Q is true; then the conjunction is false and the disjunction true. 3. P true, Q false. 4. No; the converse is a different rule.

14 · Common Mistakes

MisstepCorrection
“I do not know whether it is true, so it is not a proposition.”Unknown to you is different from having no truth value.
Reading P ∨ Q as “one but not both.”Logical ∨ is true if both are true.
Reading P → Q as Q → P.Write the premise first, then the conclusion; test the converse separately.
Calling the false-premise rows violations.The conditional puts a requirement on Q only when P is true.
Equating a working truth table with a working real sensor.A logical model assumes the stated input values; hardware observations can be inaccurate.

17 · Challenge Problem

A smart lock uses two rules: “If the card is valid, the indicator turns green” and “If the indicator is green, the door unlocks.” Let V, G, and U stand for these three statements. Translate both rules. If a log records V=T, G=T, U=F, identify which rule is violated. If the log only records U=T, can you infer that the card was valid?

Reveal a reasoned solution

Rules: V→G and G→U. With V=T,G=T,U=F, V→G is true and G→U is false. From U=T alone, V need not be true; some other unlocking mechanism may exist. The two rules do not assert their converses.

18 · Summary

A proposition has a truth value even when that value is unknown to us. Negation reverses a truth value. Conjunction requires both inputs; inclusive disjunction requires at least one. An implication P → Q is false only when its premise is true and its conclusion false. Truth tables list all input combinations and expose invalid interpretations such as silently reversing a conditional.

In mathematical reasoning, state what is known, translate the rule accurately, check relevant cases, and explain what does or does not follow.
