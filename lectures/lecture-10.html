01 · Opening Scenario: How Many Options?

A student can take one of three routes to campus. For each route, there are two available travel modes. How many route-and-mode plans are possible?

Predict: Sketch the choices first. Route A can pair with either mode, giving two plans. The same is true for routes B and C. There are 2+2+2=6 complete plans, also written 3×2=6.

Central question

Are these alternative cases, consecutive stages, overlapping groups, or ordered positions? The structure of the question determines how to count.

03 · Add Counts for Separate Cases

A menu offers three soups or two salads as a single starter. No option is both a soup and a salad. To count one starter, list the disjoint cases: three soups plus two salads gives 3+2=5 choices.

|A∪B|=|A|+|B| when A∩B=∅.

Here A is the set of soup choices and B the set of salad choices. The notation says the combined set has the sum of their sizes when the groups share no item. This is the addition rule for separate cases.

Worked example

A display shows four text themes or three image themes. If every theme belongs to exactly one category, there are 4+3=7 themes. If a theme appears in both categories, adding without correction counts it twice; we address overlap in Section 06.

Quick check: “Choose one soup or one salad” describes alternatives. It does not describe a soup-and-salad meal.

04 · Multiply Counts Across Stages

Now a meal consists of one of three soups and then one of two drinks. Each soup pairs with two drinks, so there are 3×2=6 meals. We count complete pairs, not individual menu items.

First stageComplete outcomes at the second stage
Soup S₁(S₁,D₁), (S₁,D₂)
Soup S₂(S₂,D₁), (S₂,D₂)
Soup S₃(S₃,D₁), (S₃,D₂)

This is the product rule: if stage 1 has m choices and each stage-1 choice permits n stage-2 choices, there are m×n complete outcomes. We need the same number of second-stage choices for every first-stage choice in this simple form.

Unequal branches

Suppose one route offers two modes and another offers three. Count its branches separately: 2+3=5, not 2×3. Sketching a tiny list often prevents a mistaken formula.

06 · Correct a Double Count

In a small club survey, 12 students chose coding, 9 chose design, and 4 chose both. How many chose coding or design, including those who chose both?

Adding 12+9=21 counts each of the four “both” students twice. Subtract one extra copy of each:

|C∪D|=|C|+|D|−|C∩D|=12+9−4=17.

The regions are 12−4=8 coding only, 4 both, and 9−4=5 design only. This is the inclusion–exclusion rule for two sets. It counts “at least one,” not “exactly one.” Exactly one would be 8+5=13.

Quick check: If the overlap is zero, the formula becomes ordinary addition of separate cases.

07 · When Sharing Is Unavoidable

Place four cards into three boxes. Is it possible for every box to hold at most one card? Three boxes could then hold at most three cards. The fourth card forces some box to have at least two.

4 objects → 3 boxes ⇒ at least one box contains 2 or more.

This is the pigeonhole principle: more objects than boxes force at least one shared box. It guarantees that a shared box exists; it does not say which box, or that every box is occupied.

A slightly larger challenge

Seven files go into three folders. If each folder held at most two, the folders would hold at most 2+2+2=6 files. Since there are seven, at least one folder has three or more. No exact placement is needed to prove this guarantee.

Discuss: Does four cards in three boxes guarantee that exactly two share a box? No. All four cards could be in one box. The guarantee is “at least two.”

08 · Does Order Change the Outcome?

Suppose the three distinct cards are A,B,C. Choosing cards A and B for a set gives the same selection as choosing B and A. But ranking A first and B second differs from ranking B first and A second.

TaskDoes A,B differ from B,A?Why?
Select two cards to keepNoThe same two objects are present.
Assign a first and second placeYesEach position has a different role.
Choose two candidate measurementsNoThe set is unchanged by writing order.
Rank the top two measurementsYesFirst and second rank are different roles.

We will count ordered placements this week. Week 12 develops selection without order in more detail and compares the two systematically.

09 · Arrange Distinct Objects

Arrange three distinct cards A, B, C in a row. There are three choices for the first position, then two remaining choices for the second, and one for the third:

3×2×1=6 complete arrangements.

They are ABC, ACB, BAC, BCA, CAB, CBA. This is an ordered arrangement, or permutation, of distinct objects. We have counted them by stages, without needing a new formula.

Only two positions

Choose a chairperson and deputy from four distinct people. There are four choices for chairperson, then three remaining choices for deputy: 4×3=12 assignments. If the same person were allowed in both roles, the second count would change; the problem says distinct people and different roles.

For all four distinct cards in a row, the counts fall as the cards are used: 4×3×2×1=24. These stages represent different positions, and no card is reused.

11 · Major Case Study 3 Begins: Exploring Possible Feature Sets

Scenario. A learning-support team considers eight candidate measurements for a future dashboard. The task here is purely combinatorial: count possible choices and rankings under stated rules. We have no evidence that any feature predicts learning well, and counting possibilities cannot assess predictive quality.

CodeCandidate measurementCodeCandidate measurement
QQuizzes completedEExercises attempted
APractice accuracyTTime between attempts
RRevision sessionsMMissed checkpoints
HHints requestedCTopics completed

Understand: what is one outcome?

If the team selects three measurements, {Q,E,A} and {A,Q,E} represent the same chosen set. If the team ranks the top three, (Q,E,A) and (A,Q,E) are different assignments to first, second, and third place.

Represent: count a ranking by stages

For three ranked positions with no repeated measurement, there are eight choices for first, seven for second, and six for third. Thus 8×7×6=336 rankings. Do not use 336 as the number of three-feature sets: each selected set appears in several orders.

Reason with a small sample

Restrict attention temporarily to Q,E,A. For a pair, (Q,E) and (E,Q) are different ordered pairs but the same unordered selection {Q,E}. Write all ordered pairs from those three codes without repetition, then group pairs that select the same two codes. This prepares the Week 12 count from all eight features.

Case record for next week

Given: eight distinct candidate measurements listed above; no repeats within a set or ranking.

Questions to carry forward: How many two-feature subsets can be formed? How does that compare with ordered pairs? How many three-feature sets correspond to the 336 rankings? What changes if a measurement is required or two measurements cannot be used together?

Interpretation limit: A count of feature sets says how many alternatives exist, not which alternative is useful, fair, reliable, or predictive.

We will resolve these counts and restrictions in Week 12. For now the deliverable is a clear written distinction between selection and ranking, plus the small-pair grouping.

12 · Optional Colab: Enumerate Tiny Outcomes

Self-study · mathematics first Hand-list the six route-and-mode pairs and the six ordered two-card placements from A, B, C before using Python. These cells reuse lists and loops from Week 9; there are no imports.

1 · Complete two stages

The outer loop picks a route. For each route, the indented inner loop picks a mode. One printed line is one complete plan.

routes = ["A", "B", "C"]
modes = ["walk", "bus"]
for route in routes:
    for mode in modes:
        print(route, mode)

Predict six lines: A walk, A bus, B walk, B bus, C walk, C bus. The second stage has two possibilities for every first-stage choice.

2 · Ordered placements without reuse

Now the two roles are “first” and “second.” The if condition skips using the same card twice.

cards = ["A", "B", "C"]
for first in cards:
    for second in cards:
        if first != second:
            print(first, second)

Predict 3×2=6 printed placements: AB, AC, BA, BC, CA, CB. Explain why the outputs AB and BA are different rankings but describe the same two-card set. The code checks only tiny hand counts, not feature quality.

13 · Practice and Short Counting-Method Quiz

For each problem, state what one complete outcome is. Show a short reason, not only an arithmetic expression.

  1. A menu has 4 teas or 3 juices as a single drink, with no drink in both groups. How many drink choices?
  2. Choose one of 4 teas and one of 3 snacks. How many tea-snack pairs?
  3. At a workshop, 15 students chose Python, 11 chose R, and 6 chose both. How many chose at least one? How many chose exactly one?
  4. Eight objects go into three boxes. Explain why some box contains at least three objects.
  5. Arrange three distinct books P, Q, R on a shelf. Count all orders, then list them.
  6. Assign a first and second speaker from five distinct volunteers, without reusing a person. How many assignments?
  7. A library offers two digital tutorials with two language options each, or three printed guides. How many single learning-resource options are there if each digital language version counts separately and no guide is also a digital version?
  8. For case study codes Q, E, A, list the ordered pairs of distinct codes and group those with the same underlying two-feature selection. Explain why counting all ranked triples from eight codes does not directly count three-feature sets.
15-minute formative quiz: Choose one of Problems 2, 3, 4, and 6 in sequence; identify stages, overlap, a forced shared box, and ordered roles. Discuss the reasoning after submission. The feature-set case record is preparation for Week 12, not a graded claim about model quality.

15 · Summary

  • Add the counts of separate cases; multiply across stages when each first-stage choice has the stated number of continuations.
  • For two overlapping sets, subtract their intersection once: |A∪B|=|A|+|B|−|A∩B|.
  • More objects than boxes force some box to share; small capacity arguments give stronger guarantees.
  • An ordered assignment changes when roles or positions are exchanged. Distinct items cannot be reused if the task forbids it.
  • Counting candidate feature choices does not identify good features or evaluate any predictive model.

Selected Solutions

Counting Detective

1: 3+2=5 separate choices. 2: 3×2=6 soup-drink pairs. 3: 12+9−4=17 distinct students in at least one group. 4: at least 2 cards share a box, since three boxes cannot each hold at most one of four cards. 5: 4×3=12 ordered first-and-second assignments.

1–2 · Menu choices

One drink: 4+3=7. A tea-snack pair: 4×3=12.

3 · Two overlapping groups

At least one: 15+11−6=20. Exactly one: (15−6)+(11−6)=9+5=14.

4 · Pigeonhole argument

If every box held at most two, the three boxes would hold at most six objects. Since there are eight, some box must hold at least three.

5 · Three books

3×2×1=6: PQR, PRQ, QPR, QRP, RPQ, RQP.

6 · Two speakers

Five first-speaker choices and four remaining second-speaker choices give 5×4=20.

7 · Two branches

Digital choices: 2×2=4 tutorial-language pairs. Add three separate printed guides: 4+3=7.

8 · Small feature pairs

Ordered pairs: QE, QA, EQ, EA, AQ, AE. Group by the underlying selection: {Q,E} contains QE and EQ; {Q,A} contains QA and AQ; {E,A} contains EA and AE. Ranking assigns distinct positions; swapping positions can produce a new ranking of the same selected features. The full eight-feature selection count is resolved in Week 12.
