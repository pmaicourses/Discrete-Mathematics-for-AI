01 · Opening Scenario: A Door That Stayed Closed

A campus access policy says: “If a card is valid, the door opens.” You observe that the door did not open. Can you conclude that the card was not valid?

Predict first. Under the stated logical rule, yes: if a valid card always leads to an open door, a closed door rules out a valid card. The two sentences “valid card → door opens” and “door closed → card invalid” make the same claim about allowed combinations. But if the real door could jam, the policy may not describe its actual behavior. Logic tells us what follows from the assumptions, not whether the assumptions are true of every physical event.

Reasoning Lens · questions for a short proof

  1. Assume: What facts or general rule are we allowed to use?
  2. Claim: What exactly must be established?
  3. Justify: Why does each step follow?
  4. Scope: Does the argument cover every case in the stated domain?
  5. Limit: Which assumptions would need checking in a real system?
RULE
Valid card → open door
OBSERVATION
Door did not open
CONCLUSION
Card not valid, under the rule

02 · Learning Objectives and Three-Hour Class Guide

  1. Distinguish an assumption, a claim, and a justified step in a short argument.
  2. Use a truth table to test whether two statements are logically equivalent.

03 · What Counts as a Mathematical Argument?

A mathematical argument begins with assumptions and reaches a claim through justified steps. A proof explains why the claim holds for every case covered by the assumptions. A correct example may help us discover a pattern, but it cannot by itself establish a universal claim.

Three levels · familiar → system → mathematics

From a rule to a general statement

Familiar: “If I submit before the deadline, the portal accepts my work” is a rule; one accepted submission does not prove the rule always works. System: Four successful test accounts provide useful evidence about software, but not a proof of a policy for every account. Mathematics: “For every even integer n, n² is even” requires an argument for arbitrary n, not a list such as 2²=4 and 4²=16.

Proof frame: “Let n be an arbitrary even integer. By definition, n=2k for some integer k. Therefore … . Thus the claim holds for every even n.” The word arbitrary is what lets the argument cover the whole domain.

04 · When Do Two Logical Statements Mean the Same Thing?

Let P mean “the card is valid” and Q mean “the door opens.” Two compound statements are logically equivalent when their truth values agree for every possible assignment to P and Q. We write A ≡ B. This is a statement about truth values across all cases, not merely similar sounding words.

(P → Q) ≡ (¬Q → ¬P)

The expression on the right is the contrapositive: reverse the direction and negate both parts. In the door scenario, it says, “If the door does not open, the card is not valid.” The rule Q → P is the converse: “If the door opens, the card is valid.” A staff override might open the door, so the converse is a different claim.

Meaning matters: In real equipment, “door did not open” might mean the mechanism failed. The formal deduction is valid if the rule accurately models this event. Never quietly add “only valid cards can open it” to a one-way rule.

05 · A Truth Table Tests Equivalence

List the four possible pairs of truth values for P,Q. For an implication A → B, the only false case is A=T, B=F. Compare the completed columns:

TFFTFFT

FFTTTTT

The P → Q and ¬Q → ¬P columns match in all four rows. The converse differs when P=F,Q=T: an open door does not prove a valid card.

Worked row · P=F, Q=T

Here P → Q=T because P is false. Also ¬Q=F, so ¬Q → ¬P=T. But Q → P=F: it begins with a true open-door observation and incorrectly requires a valid card. This row disproves equivalence of the original and the converse.

Small extension: The inverse ¬P → ¬Q is also not generally equivalent to P → Q. The same P=F,Q=T row makes the inverse false. We will use the original and its contrapositive for this week's proofs.

06 · Direct Proof: Begin with the Assumption

A direct proof of P → Q assumes P and derives Q. For integers, an even number has the form 2k for some integer k; an odd number has the form 2k+1. Those definitions give us a useful first step.

Worked proof · implication

If an integer is even, its square is even

  1. Let n be an arbitrary even integer. Then n=2k for some integer k.
  2. Square the expression: n²=(2k)²=4k²=2(2k²).
  3. Since 2k² is an integer, n² is twice an integer. Therefore n² is even.

The calculation is not a sample value: k can be any integer. That is why it proves the implication for every even integer.

Proof strip · ungraded pair activity: Put these steps in a valid order and explain the link between each pair: “n²=2(2k²)”; “Let n be an arbitrary even integer”; “Therefore n² is even”; “n=2k for some integer k.”

07 · Proof by Contraposition

Sometimes the conclusion is hard to derive directly from the assumption. Since P → Q is equivalent to ¬Q → ¬P, we may prove the contrapositive instead. We must negate and reverse both parts, and we must say which statement we actually proved.

Worked proof · parity

If n² is even, then n is even

Target: n² even → n even. Contrapositive: n odd → n² odd.

  1. Assume n is odd, so n=2k+1 for an integer k.
  2. Then n²=(2k+1)²=4k²+4k+1=2(2k²+2k)+1.
  3. The quantity 2k²+2k is an integer. Thus n² is odd. We proved the contrapositive, hence the original implication.
Do not prove the converse by accident: To prove “n² even → n even,” showing “n even → n² even” proves a different statement. Write the original and contrapositive side by side before calculating.

08 · Proof by Contradiction: Reach an Impossible Result

To prove a claim by contradiction, temporarily assume its opposite. If that assumption leads to something impossible while the mathematical definitions remain valid, the opposite assumption must be false. This week, a very short example is enough; long formal derivations are not needed.

Worked proof · no largest even integer

Can there be a largest even integer?

  1. Suppose, for contradiction, that L is the largest even integer.
  2. Because L is even, L+2 is also even. But L+2>L.
  3. This contradicts “largest.” Therefore there is no largest even integer.

The conflicting statements are explicit: “L is largest” and “there is a larger even integer.” The argument applies no matter which even integer someone proposes as L.

Compare methods: Direct proof begins with the original assumption. Contraposition proves an equivalent if–then statement. Contradiction assumes the negation of the claim and derives a conflict. Choose a method that gives a short, clear justification.

09 · A Counterexample Can Disprove “Every”

To show “every object has property R” is false, find one object in the stated domain without R. This is a counterexample. By contrast, several agreeing examples do not prove a universal claim.

Worked challenge

“Every even integer is divisible by 4”

The integer 2 is even but is not divisible by 4. This single example disproves the universal claim. Checking 4,8,12 would support a guess but would not prove it for every even integer.

A logical truth-table row can also be a counterexample: P=F,Q=T makes P → Q true but Q → P false, disproving the claim that the two formulas are equivalent.

11 · What This Means for a Computing System

Suppose a written policy says P → Q: a valid card must open the door. A program that treats Q → P as the same policy might accept a green/open indication as proof of a valid card, even though an override could produce that indication. A truth table can expose this change of direction before the rule is implemented.

Proof is useful for checking a logical specification. Testing is still needed to see whether software and physical devices behave as specified. Neither a small set of successful tests nor a mathematical argument about an inaccurate rule guarantees a reliable real system.

14 · Common Mistakes

MisstepCorrection
Several examples establish “for every.”Examples suggest a claim; a proof must cover every case. One counterexample can refute it.
Switch P → Q to Q → P.That is the converse. Test a separating truth-table row before treating it as equivalent.
Negate only one side for a contrapositive.Reverse and negate both: ¬Q → ¬P.
Call a calculation a proof without explaining its integer form.Show that n²=2m with an explicitly integer m.
In a contradiction argument, never identify the conflict.Name the original assumption and the incompatible derived statement.
Treat a valid derivation as proof that a sensor works.Logic is conditional on the stated facts and model.

15 · Summary

Equivalent formulas agree in every truth-table row. An implication and its contrapositive are equivalent; its converse need not be. A direct proof starts from the assumption and derives the claim. A contrapositive proof proves an equivalent statement. A contradiction proof assumes the opposite and reaches an impossibility. A counterexample disproves a universal claim, while a few successful examples cannot prove it.

Selected Solutions and Explanations

Equivalence Workshop: In row order TT,TF,FT,FF, both P → Q and ¬Q → ¬P are T,F,T,T; the converse is T,T,F,T. Row FT separates the original from its converse. The counterexample to “every even integer is divisible by 4” is 2.

Proof strip: “Let n be arbitrary even” → “n=2k for integer k” → “n²=2(2k²)” → “n² is even.”

Practice 1–2: The contrapositive is “not accepted → not authenticated”; the converse is “accepted → authenticated.” The original and contrapositive are equivalent. For P=F,Q=T, the values are T,T,F; this one row disproves equivalence to the converse.

Practice 3: Let n=2k for an integer k. Then n+6=2(k+3), and k+3 is an integer; so n+6 is even. Practice 4: The contrapositive is “if n is even, then n² is even.” Section 06 proves this, so the original “n² odd → n odd” follows.

Practice 5: Assume L is the largest multiple of 5. Then L+5 is also a multiple of 5 and exceeds L, a contradiction. Practice 6: 9 is odd but not prime because 9=3·3. Positive examples such as 3,5,7 cannot cover all odd integers.
