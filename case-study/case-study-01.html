01 · Scenario: A Student-Support Decision

A small learning-support system receives checkpoint and help-request facts. It can issue a practice reminder, an invitation to a support session, an advisor notification, or access to the next activity. A staff member asks whether a proposed action is logically justified by the information on record.

The names and facts below are fictional. The aim is to audit what follows from specified information, not to diagnose a real student.

Application fieldTransparent student-support decisions
Mathematical focusPredicates, quantified rules, chains of reasoning, missing information
Course placementWeek 2 case; Week 3 proof checks and Week 16 revision
ToolHand reasoning first; optional short Python check

Learning outcomes

Represent a finite-domain policy with predicates and quantified rules; justify multi-step conclusions; distinguish a contradicted proposal from one with insufficient information; and test how a changed fact or rule affects the decision.

Investigation questions

  1. Which actions follow from the recorded facts?
  2. Which proposed action is contradicted by an explicit negative rule and fact?
  3. Where is the system unable to decide?
  4. How would one new fact change its conclusion?
  5. What is the difference between a logically permitted decision and an effective support intervention?
Central challenge. A rule does not provide a missing fact. Can this system justify an invitation for Huda when it knows she missed a checkpoint but has no record of a request?

02 · Records, Domain and Meaning

The domain is exactly four learners: D = {Amina, Bilal, Huda, Zain}. A fact in the table is stated information for this exercise. “Unknown” means the records provide neither a positive nor a negative fact. It does not mean false.

LearnerMissed checkpoint?Requested help?Completed checkpoint?
AminaYesYesUnknown
BilalUnknownUnknownYes
HudaYesUnknownUnknown
ZainYesNo, explicitly recordedUnknown

The table records Missed(Amina), Requested(Amina), Completed(Bilal), Missed(Huda), Missed(Zain), and not Requested(Zain). It does not assert that Bilal did not miss a checkpoint or that Amina did not complete it.

Scope: We reason from stated facts and rules only. In a real system, records need verification, consent, privacy safeguards and an appropriate appeals or correction process. These issues are not settled by logic alone.

03 · Represent the Rules

Let M(x) mean “x missed a checkpoint”, H(x) “x requested help”, C(x) “x completed the checkpoint”, P(x) “offer x practice”, I(x) “invite x to a support session”, N(x) “notify an advisor about x”, and A(x) “allow x to open the next activity”. Each variable x ranges over D.

RuleOrdinary languageFor every x in D
R1If a learner missed a checkpoint, offer practice.M(x) → P(x)
R2If a learner both missed a checkpoint and requested help, invite them.M(x) ∧ H(x) → I(x)
R3If a learner is invited, notify an advisor.I(x) → N(x)
R4If a learner completed the checkpoint, allow access.C(x) → A(x)
R5If the record explicitly says a learner did not request help, do not invite them under this policy.¬H(x) → ¬I(x)

For example, R2 in compact notation is ∀x ∈ D, (M(x) ∧ H(x)) → I(x). The universal sign says this is the policy for each learner in the stated domain. It does not tell us that M or H is true for every learner.

R5 is an explicit policy rule for this case. Without R5, the bare fact ¬H(Zain) would block R2 from proving an invitation but would not by itself prove ¬I(Zain). These are distinct claims.

04 · Trace One Recommendation

Proposed decision: Invite Amina and notify an advisor. First predict from the plain-language rules; then check each required condition.

  1. Available facts: M(Amina) and H(Amina).
  2. Check R2: Both conditions hold, so I(Amina) follows.
  3. Use R3: I(Amina) is now established, so N(Amina) follows.
  4. Check R1: M(Amina) also establishes P(Amina).
  5. Stop: C(Amina) is unknown, so this record does not establish A(Amina) through R4.

05 · Audit All Four Learners

“Follows” means the facts and rules establish the proposal. “Contradicted” means they establish its negation. “Not enough information” means neither direction is established. In this consistent case, those three labels are enough for the proposed decisions below.

Proposed conclusionVerdictReason
Invite AminaFollowsM and H satisfy R2.
Notify an advisor about AminaFollowsR2 gives I; I satisfies R3.
Allow Bilal accessFollowsCompleted(Bilal) satisfies R4.
Offer Huda practiceFollowsMissed(Huda) satisfies R1.
Invite HudaNot enough informationHuda's request status is unknown; neither I nor ¬I is established.

Allow Amina accessNot enough informationCompletion status unknown; R4 cannot establish access, and no rule denies it.
Policy interpretation: The system can issue the actions supported by the rules. “Not enough information” should trigger a check or a question, not a guessed negative decision.

06 · What Would Change the Decision?

New fact test: If Huda confirms a help request, add H(Huda). Then M(Huda) ∧ H(Huda) satisfies R2, and I(Huda) follows; R3 then yields N(Huda). The earlier “not enough information” verdict changes because the input facts changed.

Countermodel test: Before the new fact, imagine one possible complete record where Huda requested help and one where she did not. Both agree with the originally stated facts. In the first record, the rule can produce an invitation; in the second, R2 alone cannot. This shows why the original records did not force I(Huda).

Policy revision test: If R5 is removed, ¬H(Zain) no longer establishes ¬I(Zain) from the remaining rules. The verdict for “Invite Zain” becomes “not enough information” under the stated information, rather than “contradicted”. Rechecking a decision after changing a rule is different from silently assuming a rule.

Decision limit: An invitation follows from the policy; that result does not prove the invitation helps learning. Actual impact requires evidence beyond the logical rule.

07 · Optional Python Check: Predict → Test → Explain

First fill the verdict table by hand. The short code below checks the positive conclusions from R1–R4 and the explicit negative conclusion from R5. A set holds the names for which one predicate is recorded as true. An absent name means unknown here, except the separately recorded not_requested fact.

learners = {"Amina", "Bilal", "Huda", "Zain"}
missed = {"Amina", "Huda", "Zain"}
requested = {"Amina"}
not_requested = {"Zain"}
completed = {"Bilal"}

practice = missed.copy()                 # R1
invited = missed & requested            # R2: both facts
not_invited = not_requested.copy()       # R5
notified = invited.copy()                # R3
access = completed.copy()                # R4

print("practice:", sorted(practice))
print("invited:", sorted(invited))
print("not invited:", sorted(not_invited))
print("notified:", sorted(notified))
print("access:", sorted(access))
print("Huda invited?", "Huda" in invited)
print("Huda invitation denied?", "Huda" in not_invited)

Expected output: practice = Amina, Huda, Zain; invited and notified = Amina; not invited = Zain; access = Bilal. Both final tests for Huda print False: the system has established neither invitation nor denial. Python's Boolean False on a membership test means “not in the derived set”, not “the real-world proposition is false”.

Misconception warning: Do not treat an absent positive record as a negative fact. This code deliberately keeps requested and not_requested separate.

08 · Student Investigation Tasks

Part A · Representation and proof trail

  1. Write the domain and explain why R2 applies to every member of it without asserting everyone missed a checkpoint.
  2. Write R1 and R4 using ∀, predicates and →.
  3. Give a two-step justification for N(Amina), naming each fact and rule.

Part B · Decision record

  1. For P(Zain), I(Huda), I(Zain) and A(Amina), assign Follows, Contradicted or Not enough information, and give one reason each.
  2. A colleague says “Bilal has access, so he must have completed.” Explain whether that statement follows from R4 alone; distinguish this case's actual completion fact from the invalid converse.
  3. If H(Huda) is added, update every new conclusion about Huda.

Part C · Rule change and responsible interpretation

  1. Remove R5. What happens to the verdict on I(Zain), and why?
  2. Suggest one factual question to ask before deciding whether to invite Huda.
  3. Name two limitations of this toy decision policy and one way to check whether the recommendation actually helps.

Optional extension: Change one positive fact and one rule, create a fresh three-way decision table, and check that the new records and rules remain consistent.

Submission expectation: A one-page fact/rule diagram or table, explicit derivations, three-way verdicts with reasons, one sensitivity change and a brief limitation paragraph. Python output is optional verification, not a substitute for the reasoning.

10 · Decision and Takeaways

Supported actions: Offer practice to Amina, Huda and Zain; invite Amina and notify an advisor; allow Bilal to open the next activity. R5 explicitly rules out an invitation for Zain under this policy. Huda's invitation and Amina's access remain unresolved from the available information.

  • Predicates name properties; a domain identifies which individuals the rules cover.
  • A conclusion follows only when its conditions can be justified from facts and earlier conclusions.
  • Contradicted and not enough information are different outcomes.
  • Changing a fact or a rule requires a fresh derivation.
  • Logical correctness of a policy decision is not evidence that the policy improves learning.

Key terms: domain · predicate · universal rule · fact · implication · conclusion · countermodel · insufficient information.
